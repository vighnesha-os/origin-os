## The kernel's random-number generator (ADR-0016; TASKS 18a and 18b; 2026-11-11)

Task 18b, on `claude/18b-kernel-random` (cut from `main` at `f654d71`), built what ADR-0016 decides on top of 18a's mechanism. **Everything below has run under QEMU 11.1.3 with OVMF on its software processor, and on nothing else.** The times a boot prints are an emulator's and claim nothing.

- **One generator, and nothing else draws from the processor.** Every random value in the kernel and the loader comes from NIST's HMAC_DRBG over SHA-512. RDSEED and RDRAND are run in two files only, `framekernel/frame/src/random.rs` and `boot/uefi/src/random.rs`, in their 64-bit forms, and a gate in `cargo xtask check` holds that. `framekernel/src/entropy.rs` and `cpu::rdrand` and `cpu::rdseed` are gone.
- **A root, and a generator for each processor.** The boot processor starts both before another processor starts; each application processor starts its own as it comes up. A seed is made of 1,024 start-up draws of each instruction that passes its tests.
- **Health tests on what the instructions return** (`origin-random`): zero and all ones set aside, a repeat, a window's first draw coming again, a bit that never moves, and a start-up test of 1,024 draws. They catch gross failure only, and Origin claims no SP 800-90B conformance.
- **Steps.** Every piece of the generator's work runs with interrupts off on a stack of the generator's own, one for each processor, which is overwritten whole before interrupts come back on. So the longest time it keeps them off is one step, whatever the retry budget, and a draw never rests on a thread's stack. A request makes at most 16 draws. A dry RDSEED rests for a second, and the reseed is made from RDRAND at once.
- **The loader** has a generator of its own: two slides from two requests, and 64 bytes handed to the kernel in boot info 0.6, which the kernel mixes into the root's first seed and overwrites where they lay.
- **With no healthy instruction there is no generator**: every request is refused, no process is launched, the network asks for no lease, and the boot still ends with a verdict that names it. "Hardware entropy" is now the Withdraw row "random-number generator" (ADR-0013 Amendment 1).
- **Consumers on `main`:** where a process's start record goes, and the DHCP and TFTP identifiers.
- **Evidence:** 55 host tests of the policy, with seeds checked byte for byte against an independent Python reference and 68 of 68 planted faults caught; the mechanism's tests from 18a, NIST's 30 vectors among them; boots of the standard machine, of a processor with RDRAND only and of one with neither instruction; the controls C37 to C42 in ADR-0013. A boot measures that a request uses 3,024 of the 8,192 bytes of the generator's stack and that only zeros are on it afterwards.
- **Not built:** the loader's call to the firmware's random-number protocol (left to a session that has not read EDK II's declaration of it, decision item 92); the three places on `land/s3-part2` that still draw from the processor, which must ask `origin_frame::random` when the branches meet; reseeding on resume and after a restored snapshot; an interface for programs.
- **Not verified:** anything on a real processor: what RDSEED and RDRAND cost, how often they are not ready, how many processors asking at once behave, and whether the provisional budgets (a 64-byte request under 5 µs, no step over 10 µs after a processor's start) are met. An NMI or a machine check in the middle of a step saves registers on its own stack, which is not overwritten. No human has reviewed any of it. The evidence is in `docs/engineering/SOURCE_PROVENANCE.md`, "The random-number generator in the kernel and the loader".

## Address spaces that change while they run (ADR-0011 N14 step 3, task S3-5a; 2026-11-10)

The framework's half of step 3's memory work, with no system call yet (S3-5b adds `memory_map`, `memory_unmap` and `memory_protect` on top of it):

- **The kernel half is fixed at boot (ADR-0004 H2).** Every top-level entry of the kernel half gets its table before the kernel's tables go live, so a kernel mapping made after a process exists is visible from that process. Before this, each process copied the entries that existed when it was created, a defect hidden only because the proof processes ended before PCIe and MMIO mapping.
- **A process's address space can change while its threads run on other processors** (`origin_frame::userspace::UserSpace`): map, unmap and protect, under an editor lock held across the shootdown and a short state lock that never is.
- **Frames are freed only after a completed TLB shootdown**, also a frame shared between two address spaces (`SharedFrame`), and a shootdown for a user mapping interrupts only the processors that have that space loaded. A processor that does not answer leaks the frame instead of stopping the kernel.
- **What each change costs is reported** (`Change`), for S3-5b to charge or limit a program that maps and unmaps in a loop.

**What is proven, under QEMU 11.1 TCG:** boot proofs, each a Refuse row in ADR-0013: the kernel half, with a control in every boot (a space lacking the slot faults); a page unmapped under a reading thread on another processor is free when the unmap returns and is reused with a secret the thread never reads (and a reader that still maps it does read it, in every boot); permissions withdrawn and added under running threads; a page mapped under one; 5 and 200 pages at once; a frame in two spaces; 64 map-and-unmap rounds that interrupt only the processor in the space. Six edits to the framework each fail the boot (ADR-0013, C18 to C23). The shootdown protocol and the deferred free are the code the Loom models run, with committed negative controls. **What is not:** physical hardware; a human review of the new safe APIs; any limit on a hostile loop (S3-5b); the spurious fault a permission upgrade prevents, which QEMU cannot show. Threat model: `docs/engineering/THREAT_MODEL_ADDRESS_SPACES.md`.

**Its independent review (one AI reviewer, 2026-11-10)** found seven issues; findings 1–5 and 7 and the housekeeping are fixed on `claude/s3-5a-review-fixes`. A space's record and the CR3 write now change together with interrupts off; only the scheduler activates a space, and proofs visit one instead; a stalled shootdown is remembered until a flush completes; the kernel-half proof's probe page is no longer global, the defect CI's run on a real processor exposed, and CI's next run passed on one (`81cb61c`, an AMD EPYC 7763 under KVM); W^X holds across every mapping of a shared frame; and the Loom controls fail with their property's own message. Finding 6, an unbounded wait, is documented and unchanged. The owner settled ADR-0004 H2's table as Amendment 1 (decision sheet items 98–100): the heap stays in the direct map, the stacks move to their own region with guard pages that take no memory (not built yet), and slot 480 holds only the probe page. Each fix by finding: the threat model and `SOURCE_PROVENANCE.md`.

## Speculation and processor hardening (ADR-0017; TASKS item 19; 2026-11-10)

**Decided:** `docs/adr/ADR-0017-speculation-processor-hardening.md` and `docs/engineering/THREAT_MODEL_SYSTEM.md`, both accepted by the owner (decision items 124 to 141), from a ledger of Intel's and AMD's own documents read that day. Amendment 1 (item 152): the toolchain's prebuilt libraries are outside the image rules, by name.

**Built (task 19b) on branch `claude/intelligent-dhawan-a7f6d4`, and merged into the review batch (`land/review-tasks`, pull request 15) on 2026-11-10:**

- Every index, count and address a program chooses goes through one framework primitive, `origin_frame::nospec`, before the kernel looks anything up or copies anything with it: a conditional move, or LFENCE, by the processor's maker and family. The list is in `THREAT_MODEL_SYSCALLS.md`.
- Each processor reads what it reports as it starts, sets the controls it enumerates (enhanced IBRS with `BHI_DIS_S` and `RRSBA_DIS_S`, or automatic IBRS with STIBP), reads them back and records the result. The boot prints what is on and what the processor lacks, and never refuses on it.
- An IBPB when a processor takes up a different process.
- The trap entry zeroes the general-purpose registers once it has saved them.
- The kernel is built with no jump tables and an INT3 after every return, and `cargo xtask check` reads the built image to hold both, and the entry's register clearing.

**What is shown, and it is little:** host tests of the decision against AMD's published register values for two family 1Ah models and against Intel values built from the SDM's bit positions; boot proofs under QEMU 11.1.3's software processor. **That processor has none of these controls**, so every boot there prints `on: nothing` and ends `degraded: 58 of 59 proofs passed; failed: speculation controls` (kept so by the owner, decision item 154), and **no control has ever been written on a processor that has it.** CI's boots under KVM did not change that. On 2026-11-11 the review batch's CI ran its real-CPU boots at `55db1f1` on an AMD EPYC 7763 (family 25) and an Intel Xeon Platinum 8573C (family 6, model 207), each as a guest under KVM with `-cpu max`. The guests offered none of the controls: every boot printed `on: nothing; ... not offered: enhanced or automatic IBRS, IBPB, branch isolation between sibling threads` and `this processor has no IBPB to measure`. The fence form was the one in use in one of those boots, the x2APIC scenario, which presents an Intel vendor name with family 25, and it passed. No test can show what a processor does on a wrongly guessed path. No processor in the declared scope has been run (§74).

**Not built:** FRED, LASS, shadow stacks, the nested-NMI guard (row E12 of the system-call threat model stays Open) and the per-thread `SPEC_CTRL` value: task 19c. Direct calls between the kernel's crates: TASKS item 33 (decision item 153).

## The Framekernel framework crate (ADR-0011 N6, N14 step 1; 2026-11-09)

The kernel now has one privileged crate, `framekernel/frame/`, and `cargo xtask check` fails if `unsafe` appears in the kernel outside it or a kernel dependency runs the wrong way. There is none outside it: 351 sites (by the gate's count, of `unsafe` tokens and `global_asm!`; the review's earlier count was 310) became 0, and the allowlist that tracked the migration is gone. Above the framework is one library, `framekernel`, with `#![forbid(unsafe_code)]` at its root: drivers, services and the boot flow are safe code on typed framework interfaces (`PhysAddr`, `Iova`, `MmioRegion`, `DmaPage`, `OwnedFrame`, `UserSpace`, `GuardedStack`, `PortRange`), device interrupts and the scheduler's upcalls are registered (`origin_frame::irq`, `origin_frame::trap`), and early boot is a capability (`origin_frame::early`) whose steps run once and refuse out of turn. The AP trampoline, IOMMU programming and page-table set-up are in the framework, as N12 lists them. The image's link root, `framekernel/frame/entry/main.rs`, holds `_start`. Not done: the OIDL generator and the rest of ADR-0011, and no independent review of the framework's soundness arguments, whose evidence is in `docs/engineering/SOURCE_PROVENANCE.md`. Everything still runs under QEMU TCG + OVMF only.

Only firmware can close the first (pre-boot DMA protection; T5). T4 is **Partly** closed: threat model.
- NVMe and virtio-net run all their existing proofs through domains of their own. Their IOVAs start at 1 GiB and are never physical addresses; virtio-net gets there through `VIRTIO_F_ACCESS_PLATFORM`.
- Every MSI-X entry of both devices is a handle to an IRTE that checks the device's source ID. Under remapping an APIC ID above 255 is accepted: the old refusal is lifted. Since the CPU-index work this is proven live: in `boot-x2apic` the NVMe MSI-X vectors routed to APIC ID 256 wake threads on that processor.
- I/O APIC pins are routed through IRTEs too (2026-11-08), because hardware drops compatibility-format I/O APIC interrupts once remapping is on. The redirection entry is in remappable format, naming an IRTE on the unit whose DMAR scope lists the I/O APIC. The IRTE has the pin's trigger mode and checks the I/O APIC's source ID (0xff00 in QEMU's DMAR). The keyboard IRQ can now take the same path. Under `boot-x2apic` the pin is routed to the processor at APIC ID 256. Under AMD-Vi the pins are not routed yet: the I/O APIC's device table entry blocks them, so they stay masked, and the boot says so.
- Proven at boot:
  - a DMA through an IOVA lands in the physical page it maps;
  - after an unmap (the IOTLB invalidated and drained), the same DMA is blocked, the page left untouched, and recorded (source ID, address, write, reason 5);
  - an MSI through a not-present IRTE is blocked and recorded (reason 22h);
  - the PIT's IRQ0 arrives through its IRTE on another CPU (CPU 0 at `-smp` 1), with the entry and the IRTE read back. With the IRTE made not present, nothing arrives and the unit records reason 22h; restored, the interrupts resume.
- Negative controls fail the boot for each of: no IOTLB invalidation, device pages not mapped, a device without a context entry, IRE off, a wrong source ID in the IRTEs, and a probe IRTE left present. For the I/O APIC: a compatibility-format entry (caught even with the read-backs disabled), an IRTE never written (with the read-back disabled: no interrupts, reason 22h), a probe that leaves the IRTE present, and a wrong source ID or trigger mode in the pin's IRTE.
- **QEMU cannot show the source-ID check on I/O APIC interrupts.** Its I/O APIC sends messages without a requester ID, so its VT-d records their faults as 0xffff and never checks their source ID; the boot measures this and says so. Hardware must show reason 26h. CFI blocking and the interrupt-entry-cache invalidation need hardware too.
- QEMU's VT-d only.

**AMD-Vi (the same ADR-0007, AMD-Vi section; `ORIGIN_QEMU_IOMMU=amd`):**

## The interface language (ADR-0011 N7, step 2 of N14): **accepted by the owner, and the kernel's system-call layer is generated from it**

`docs/spec/OIDL.md` specifies OIDL: its grammar, types, errors, versioning rules, declared capabilities and the fixed-layout wire format (little-endian, aligned, handles out of band as position slots, booleans as `u8` of 0 or 1, exact size at the negotiated minor, closed enums and bits, zero padding, a hello on every connection). **The owner accepted the format on 2026-11-09**, the recommended answer to each of the twelve questions in `docs/spec/OIDL.md` §19 (§84).

`developer-tools/oidl` is a host-only tool: a checked parser and validator, Rust and C generators, reference documentation, a per-minor ABI fingerprint with a lock gate, and a hostile-message corpus labelled by an independent model. The kernel's 11 system calls are defined in `sdk/interfaces/origin.kernel.oidl`. **The server side of that definition is committed in the kernel-private crate `framekernel/syscalls`, and the kernel uses it (below); the clients, the C header and `origin.core`'s stubs are still generated into a scratch directory only.**

**What is proven, on the host only:** every malformed-definition rejection is tested; 40,000 seeded byte and token mutations of the definitions leave the tool unpanicked; the generated code compiles for the host and both freestanding targets, passes clippy with the no-panic lints and holds no `unsafe` outside two named shims; each system-call stub has exactly one `syscall` instruction and its own number, by disassembly; the corpus (12,960 cases) passes, and each of 34 planted faults in the generated checks fails it; a channel client and server round-trip over a loopback, with every refusal path and the specification's worked example byte for byte. **Comparison with the hand-written ABI** (`docs/engineering/OIDL_KERNEL_COMPARISON.md`): every value matches, and 2.26 million register sets give the same status from the generated dispatch and from a transcription of the kernel's argument handling; one narrow precedence difference is reported. **Not done:** nothing runs on a real channel, a channel service's performance has not been measured, the channel transport has no C entry points (`origin.core`'s call helper does not exist), and `channel_read` does not report a received handle's type and rights, so the rights check on channel handles cannot be enforced yet (question Q8).

**Task S3-0 (2026-11-09, ADR-0011 N14 step 3): the kernel's call table, argument decoding and statuses come from the definition, with no ABI change.** `framekernel/syscalls` (written by `cargo xtask regenerate`, from `oidl generate --server-only`) holds the generated types and server side and neither `unsafe` shim: its root forbids `unsafe`, and the no-panic object check covers it. `framekernel::syscall` implements the generated `Calls` and converts at that boundary, so `ipc.rs`, `handles.rs` and `channel.rs` did not change; the range pin and the reaping stay in `run`. `cargo xtask check` now fails if that code is not what the definition generates, or if the ABI is not the one `sdk/interfaces/origin.kernel.lock` records, even at major 0 (`developer-tools/xtask/src/interfaces.rs`). `origin-abi` keeps every public item, and a host test holds them equal to the generated values. One behaviour changed, on purpose (`docs/engineering/OIDL_KERNEL_COMPARISON.md`, item 5): `handle_duplicate` refuses an undefined right before it looks at the out pointer.

**What is proven:** the 117 hostile calls and every `ipc:` and `user mode:` boot line pass unchanged, in `boot-matrix` under QEMU's software CPU, and the lines read the same as `main`'s. Negative controls, each in a scratch copy: the definition edited without regenerating fails `cargo xtask check` at the freshness gate; `@nonzero` removed from `wait_one`'s signals and regenerated fails the boot (`no signals -> TIMED_OUT`); and the WRITE check removed from `channel_write`'s path fails the boot (`write without WRITE -> OK`). That last one is run at the check in `ipc.rs`, because the kernel's `Calls` does not read `need_rights`. The ping-pong round trip, as QEMU figures with no budget (rule 21): a median of 61.1 µs before and 59.8 µs after, over six interleaved boots of each kernel on QEMU's software CPU (59.7 to 62.0 µs with one boot at 85.4, and 56.0 to 66.4 µs): no difference that six boots can resolve. `origin-abi`'s call numbers stay until step 4 moves them out of the stable crate.

**Task S3-4 (2026-11-10, ADR-0011 N14 step 3): the service directory.** The owner's decisions C11 and C12 (ADR-0005 Amendment 4) and ADR-0011 N9. `not_found = -14` is appended to the statuses (`origin.kernel` 0.4, `user-abi` 0.4). `origin.directory` 0.1 is the directory's interface: one two-way method, `connect(interface_id, major, server)`, whose errors are `not_found` and `limit`; the server end must be a channel with read, write, wait and transfer. Its crate, `sdk/directory`, is generated by a new mode of the tool (`oidl generate --library`) and holds its server and client and `origin.kernel`'s types, with no `unsafe`; its own lock records 0.0 and 0.1, and the interface gate now checks every generated crate against its lock. The lookup (`services/directory`) passes a connect's end to the provider the launcher gave the directory for that interface and major, then answers ok; for a key it does not hold it answers `not_found`, and for a provider that cannot take the end now `limit`, closing the end either way, and it keeps nothing past the call. A §16 ledger (`docs/research/2026-11-10-service-directory.md`) and the threat rows (D1–D11 in `docs/engineering/THREAT_MODEL_SYSCALLS.md`) came before the code. **What is proven:** on the host, nine tests drive the lookup through the generated client and stub, among them that an end without `transfer`, or an event, never reaches the directory; in ring 3, a new boot proof, `the service directory` (Refuse), with its own leak check: a launcher starts a directory, a service and a client; the client's connect, and a request it writes on its own end before any reply, reach the service through the directory, the request stamped with the client's identity, not the directory's; the client reads the service's reply stamped with the service's; and it gets ok, then `not_found` (-14) for a major the directory does not hold, whose end reports `peer_closed`. Every message is one the generated client or stub made. Seven negative controls each fail the boot. **Also:** the generated client's hello is now `new`, which frees `connect` as a method name for C12; and `docs/spec/OIDL.md` §7.4 no longer says `channel_read` lacks handle records. **Not done:** how a launcher fills a directory and how a provider receives connections are not decided (D11, step 4): the proof uses an arrangement of its own; the ring-3 directory and service are scripts until step 4 brings Rust there; fairness between a directory's clients; and what a client checks a service's identity against (ADR-0011 S9).

**Task S3-3 (2026-11-10, ADR-0011 N14 step 3): a process starts from its start record.** The owner's decisions C1 and C4–C6 (ADR-0005 Amendment 4) shape P6. One launch path in the kernel makes a creator's child, which acts for the creator's principal and cannot be given another. It places the child's start record, version 1, on read-only pages at a random address drawn from the processor's entropy, and never at a predictable one; gives the child its own process and first thread with C6's rights and the BOOTSTRAP and DIRECTORY channel ends its creator passes; writes the record from what the child's table then holds (a 32-byte header, a 16-byte entry per handle with its type, rights and role, then the arguments and environment); and starts the first thread with RDI holding the record's address. A launch that fails after the child holds anything gives everything back. `origin-abi` has the codec and `origin.kernel` 0.3 the layout, roles (4, SERVICE, retired) and rights, which a host test holds together. A §16 ledger (`docs/research/2026-11-10-start-record.md`) and the threat rows (P1–P10 in `docs/engineering/THREAT_MODEL_SYSCALLS.md`) came before the code. **What is proven:** a new boot proof, `process start` (Refuse), with its own leak check: two children read their records back in ring 3, use each handle, write a message on BOOTSTRAP that names their creator's principal, and fault writing their record; the records are at different addresses; a launch over 64 KiB is refused, and one refused after its child held a handle gives everything back. Seven negative controls each fail the boot on their own line. **Also:** OIDL now refuses, from major 1, an item that names one of its library's items from a later minor, which the review of S3-2 asked to decide; and the batch's merge of S3-2 under S3-5a, which left one boot proof calling `Process::new` without a principal, is fixed. **Not done:** process creation from ring 3 (the Loader Service), the directory DIRECTORY will name (S3-4), random bytes for programs, and CET at entry.

**Task S3-2 (2026-11-10, ADR-0011 N14 step 3): a message names its writer, and a received handle its type and rights.** The owner's decisions C1–C3 (ADR-0005 Amendment 4) shape B1 and B3. Every process acts for a principal its creator gives and nothing changes. When a message is written, the kernel stamps it with the writing process's ID and principal, taken from the process and never from an argument, so a writer cannot set them; a message keeps its stamp while its channel end moves to another process. `channel_read` reports the writer through `read_sizes`, now 32 bytes, as far as the caller's `size` reaches: a caller that gives the old 12 gets the counts, nothing past them, and no error, and a read too small for its message still names the writer. Each handle received is a record of its value, object type and rights, so a receiver can check a handle before using it. OIDL now allows plain structs of integers, enums and bits as system-call vector elements, encoded field by field. `user-abi` is 0.2 and the fingerprint lock is re-recorded. A §16 ledger (`docs/research/2026-11-10-caller-identity.md`) came first, and the system-call boundary has a threat model (`docs/engineering/THREAT_MODEL_SYSCALLS.md`). **What is proven:** a new boot proof, `a message's writer` (Refuse), with its own leak check: three processes with their own principals, a message written before its end moved and two after, a too-small read and an old-size read; the IPC proof's record lines and a hostile call with room for only part of a record; 10 negative controls, each failing the boot on its own line. The ping-pong round trip as QEMU figures: a median of 60.7 µs before and 63.5 µs after, over six interleaved boots of each kernel (59.4 to 65.4 µs and 57.7 to 78.8 µs), inside the boot-to-boot spread; the round trip's path gained a 16-byte copy per message. **Not done:** where a channel stub puts the identity waits for the first ring-3 service (step 4), and program identity is a later minor. **Found and fixed:** the fingerprint named a method's sized struct with its newest size, so growing `read_sizes` changed the 0.0 and 0.1 fingerprints too; harmless at major 0, it would have refused an allowed addition after. At the owner's request the reference now carries the size at the minor described, and those two minors are back to their values before the growth.

**Task S3-1 (2026-11-09, ADR-0011 N14 step 3): processes and threads are objects, with no ABI change.** A handle can name a process or a thread; every lookup states the rights and the object type it needs, and for a system call both come from the definition through the decoder, so the table is the only place that checks them and `ipc.rs` names no right (`docs/spec/OIDL.md` §6). A process or thread handle gets `WRONG_TYPE` from a channel call. `wait_one` waits on any object. Each raises `TERMINATED`: a thread when it ends, a process once its handles have closed and its memory has gone (ADR-0005 P11's order). A process's address space now goes when the last of its threads has been reaped, whoever still holds a handle to the process; each process has a 64-bit ID, unique for the boot and never reused (C1); and a status a call does not declare no longer passes unseen: it is counted, the caller still gets a failure it can report, and the IPC boot proof fails. Kernel objects are allocated through `ObjectRef`, so running out of memory is an error, not a stopped kernel. **What is proven:** a new boot proof, `process and thread objects` (Refuse, ADR-0013's table), with its own leak check, and 12 negative controls each failing as intended, among them a `Calls` implementation that ignores `need_rights` and the definition edited to stop asking for `write` with no kernel change. The ping-pong round trip as QEMU figures: a median of 62.7 µs before and 61.6 µs after, over six interleaved boots of each kernel on QEMU's software CPU (60.3 to 79.9 µs and 58.6 to 68.3 µs): no difference that six boots can resolve. **Not done:** `TERMINATED` on a process waits for its last thread to be reaped, so a processor kept busy by a ring-3 program that never enters the kernel delays it, as it delays the reaping of any thread's stack; nothing creates a process or thread from a system call yet (S3-3); and a process cannot terminate another one (`manage` waits for a framework call to kill a blocked thread).

## Parser hardening (CLAUDE.md §13, §72)

Every parser in the host-tested library that reads firmware or device data has a deterministic, seeded mutation soak on `crate::testing`: the ACPI tables (RSDP, XSDT, FADT, MADT, SRAT, SLIT, MCFG, HPET), PCI configuration space and capabilities, NVMe registers and Identify, GPT, the CMOS RTC, virtio capabilities and rings, CPUID decoders, the boot info, the archive and filesystem formats and the network parsers. Each soak checks that nothing panics (overflow checks on), nothing reads out of bounds, and anything accepted keeps its documented invariants, round-tripping where an encoder exists. Checksummed structures are re-sealed so the checks behind them are reached. A default run is about 1.3 million mutated inputs and takes 1 s. The soaks found 13 bugs in the library (a CPUID overflow, an on-disk arithmetic overflow, device-reported virtqueue ids that panicked, virtio capabilities read past the capability area and taken in the wrong order, and others) and kernel paths that trusted what the parsers returned (structures and MSI-X tables outside their memory BARs, a queue size that was not a power of two, doorbells past BAR 0). Each is fixed with a regression test, and each test is shown to fail with its fix reverted. The malformed-device paths in the kernel binary can only be shown on a fault-injecting device model or hardware (§74). The full inventory and per-parser results are in `docs/engineering/SOURCE_PROVENANCE.md`.

The ONBF parsers, which run in the boot loader on data read before any signature is checked, have their own seeded multi-fault soaks: one to four faults per case over the container, OCBOR-1, the KRN manifest and the signature block, plus Merkle and signature verification, each judged by a model written from the specification (re-encoding byte for byte where an encoder exists, and compared in both directions). A default run is about 98,000 cases and adds about 1 s to `cargo xtask check`. They found five conformance bugs, none a panic or an out-of-bounds read, since all of these crates are panic-free by lint and by the object check: a mapped MERKLE or DWARF section was accepted, a certificate chain longer than four, a statement version that is not SemVer, and a block larger than 1 MiB passed straight to `SignatureBlock::parse`. Each is fixed with a regression test and shown to fail with its fix reverted.

## Concurrency models (CLAUDE.md §73; 2026-11-08)

`cargo xtask loom`, also part of `cargo xtask check`, model-checks the real `SpinLock`, the real `RwLock`, the real wait-queue protocol and the real run queue under Loom 0.7.2, from a scratch workspace with its own committed lock file: the root `Cargo.lock` holds only workspace packages, so building the kernel needs no network, and `cargo xtask check` fails if a third-party package appears there. To run under Loom the protocol moved out of the kernel binary, unchanged, into `origin_sync::wait`.

**Proved, within each model's printed bounds** (26 models, each with negative controls: 35 in all; every interleaving up to Loom's reduction, in Loom's model of C11): one `SpinLock` holder at a time, no data race on the protected value, and each holder sees earlier holders' writes whole; `try_lock` likewise; an `RwLock` writer overlaps nobody, readers can hold it together and see its updates whole; and **no lost wake-up**: a waiter whose condition is made true by a thread that then calls `wake_one` does not stay blocked, even with `Relaxed` on the condition (the queue's lock carries the ordering), a wait returns only with its condition true, a timed-out waiter leaves no registration behind, threads that cannot register still see the condition by polling, and a timed wait on the boot flow polls and ends with the condition or the deadline. 22 negative controls (weakened orderings, a removed wake-up, an early wake-up forgotten by the run queue, a condition read outside the lock, and others), each applied to a scratch copy of the source, make the models fail with the expected report; every model has one.

**Not proved:** anything past the bounds; what Loom's memory model omits (load buffering, spurious `compare_exchange_weak` failures); interrupts, the context switch, IPIs and timers, which the models stand in for; fairness and starvation; the x86-64 machine code; a timed waiter that finds the queue full. The full statement, the bounds and the 30 approved third-party crates with their licences and sizes are in `docs/engineering/SOURCE_PROVENANCE.md`, "Loom models".

## Boot proofs under host load (CLAUDE.md §68, §72)

Every boot proof that times one clock against another now tolerates a loaded host. Under a loaded host a virtual CPU is descheduled for milliseconds, and each such proof read that as the clock being wrong:

- the RTC second: updates are seen through the seconds register, which cannot be missed, instead of the 244 µs update-in-progress pulse QEMU shows (a pulse was missed at 29 % of updates under load), and each is located between the polls around it (`rtc::UpdateWatcher`). A measurement decides only if its whole interval of uncertainty is inside or outside 1 % (`rtc::SecondMeter`), so a stall costs a measurement, never a wrong verdict; a vague pair gives way to the next edge, at most 3.2 s in all.
- the PIT calibration of the TSC: a 10 ms window with a stall at either end (or between programming the count and raising the gate) is recognised and measured again (`apic::CalibrationWindow`); before, such stalls corrupted the TSC rate by up to 39 %, which was behind all but one of the `one RTC second lasted … clock failed` boots.
- the cross-CPU TSC check keeps going, to 500 ms at most, until the processors have really interleaved; the HPET and PM-timer reads are paired tightly with the TSC (`clock::closest_read`); and `prove_timers` repeats a run in which the polling loop itself stalled.

With every host core loaded (10 busy-loop processes, 30 boots each, alternating boot by boot), `cargo xtask boot-test`'s failure rate went from 83 % (the original kernel) to 3 % (this one: 1 boot in 30, the scheduler's weighted-share bound, untouched here). RTC-wait timeouts went from 11 of 30 boots to 0. Twelve negative controls fail the boot: frozen and stuck RTCs, an RTC 3 % slow, an RTC at 2× and at ½ speed, a PIT rate 3 % high, a corrupted PIT window accepted, an AP's TSC skewed by 5000 ticks, handoffs unreachable, the HPET and PM timer 3 % fast, and a timer genuinely late while the poller runs. Three positive controls show a stall is forgiven, not hidden: an injected stall in a PIT window, in an HPET read and in a timer run is each detected and retried, and the boot passes. Evidence: `docs/engineering/SOURCE_PROVENANCE.md`, "The clock proofs under host load".

The scheduler's weighted-share proof (weights 1, 4 and 16 on one processor for 2 s), the last boot proof still failing at one `yes` per logical core, has the same treatment. It counted loop iterations, and on a loaded host the iterations in a 10 ms slice vary by more than tenfold whatever the scheduler did, while the lightest thread's share is the sum of about ten slices; so each worker now reports the time it saw itself running (steps under 3 ms), with the bound unchanged and pinned by a host test. A run whose shares are wrong although its processor visibly lost timer ticks or was away for over a tenth of the window is measured again, at most three runs in all; a wrong result from an undisturbed run fails at once. Over 150 runs of the proof per measure with every host core loaded, the old iteration count broke the bound once and the new measure never; at double load (two `yes` per core) 9 times (6 %) and twice (1.3 %, both visibly disturbed, so repeated). In `cargo xtask boot-test`, alternating the two kernels boot by boot, neither failed in 30 boots at one `yes` per core; at two per core the old kernel failed this proof in 2 boots of 15 and this change in none (`timers`, a different proof, failed one boot of each). Negative controls fail the boot (a wrong weight, weights ignored, a worker never picked, a wrong pick, no preemption, and a step limit so large that preemptions count), and a 70 ms stall injected into a run is repeated and passes, and fails if the repeat is disabled. Evidence: `docs/engineering/SOURCE_PROVENANCE.md`, "The weighted-share proof under host load".

The two proofs still failing at double load (two `yes` per logical core) have had the same treatment. `prove_timers` now brackets every firing between the polls around it, and a run decides only if each firing's whole interval is inside or outside the 2 ms limit (`timers::judge`): a firing the queue held past the limit, or a wrong order, fails at once, and a run a stall left undecided is measured again, up to 64 runs (six before; a run is about 15 ms and an undisturbed boot still pays for one). In 36,000 firings at up to four `yes` per core none was decisively late, so every lateness the old proof reported was a late look; at double load the six-run budget ran out for 4.4 % of the starting points in 3600 runs, and 24 runs never ran out. The ping-pong between two processors (1000 round trips) still passes only if a run's mean round trip is under 2000 µs, the old bound. A run over it is measured again if the boot flow's own loop went without a turn for at least a tenth of the run, at most 8 runs in all; without that reading it fails at once. The reading only ever earns a repeat, because a thread on that processor can produce it just by running. The first version, `05deb69`, used it to swap the mean for the median and the share of reschedule IPIs; a review showed a kernel that delays itself passing that rule, and "the bound is unchanged" was not true of such a run, so it was replaced. A version judged on lost timer ticks on both processors was built and rejected too: a kernel holding interrupts off on both processors passes it. The mean is the host's tail on such a run: at double load the slowest tenth of the round trips carries 60 % of the time.

In `cargo xtask boot-test`, alternating the final rule against `118834b` boot by boot, no boot of either kernel failed the ping-pong at 2× (means of 1287 to 1722 µs) and the old kernel failed the timer proof in 1 of the 14 boots that reached it (`worst lateness 3111 us`) where the final rule passed all 14 (7 after a stalled run). Neither failed either proof at 1×. At 4× the host's ordinary round trip in this batch is over the bound: the final rule passed 2 of the 6 boots that reached the ping-pong, after repeats, and failed 4 after all 8 runs; the old kernel failed all 5 it reached. There the proof is measuring the host and says so by failing; most boots at 4× stopped earlier anyway, in the APIC timer and clock proofs, not touched here. The same kernel took about 2.5 times as long per round trip in this batch as in the day's earlier ones, so a count of `yes` does not by itself fix the stress. Also seen and not touched: the RTC proof once at 1×, the timed wait (81 ms, bound 80) once at 2×, and the iteration-counted two-worker fairness check in 2 boots of the final kernel at 2× and 4×, 2 of 105 against 0 of 105 for the old kernel, which is too few to tell from chance. Negative controls fail the boot: a timer 3 ms late with the poller running, a cancelled timer firing, a queue that never fires; for the ping-pong, the pinger or the ponger delaying itself 10 ms on every fourth round trip, holds with interrupts off on either or both sides, no reschedule IPI, every second or fourth IPI lost, a 3 ms slow wake path, and on one processor every fourth round trip 10 ms slow. Positive controls: a 9 ms stall across a timer deadline, and a 2.5 s stall of one or both processors in the first run, are repeated and pass, and fail with the repeat disabled. Everything is QEMU TCG. Evidence: `docs/engineering/SOURCE_PROVENANCE.md`, "The wait-queue ping-pong and the timer queue under host load".

On the merged IOMMU branch (`31b4347`), `cargo xtask boot-matrix` passed in full on its first attempt with the host at load 50–260 (mean 168) and another session's QEMU running for most of it: 8 kernel boots, one `timers` run repeated after a stall, and 41 calibration windows discarded over 6 of those boots. Five attempts on the tree before this merge failed at load 57–170 or with another session's boots running. That is one run, not a failure rate.

With the early console and the wait-queue and timer fixes merged (`31c3d39`), `cargo xtask check` passes (827 tests, 0 failures, 6 ignored) and `cargo xtask boot-matrix` passes on a quiet host (load 2.7–4.0), on its first attempt: 8 kernel boots and the 8 console boots. All 15 ping-pongs passed at their first run (41 to 56 µs on two or more processors, 5 µs on one), and all 15 `prove_timers` runs decided at their first run (worst lateness 0 µs in 14, 38 µs in one). Drawing a console line took 226 to 245 µs on average and at most 808 µs, under QEMU TCG. That is one run on a quiet host and says nothing about double load, where the measurements above stand: at four busy-loop processes per core the ping-pong still fails most of the boots that reach it.
