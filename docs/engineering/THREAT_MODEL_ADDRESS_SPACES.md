## What is being protected

- What a frame holds, from anyone who is not its current owner. A frame moves between owners when one space lets it go and the allocator gives it to another.
- The kernel's own mappings: every address space must reach the same kernel.
- The kernel's progress: a process's map and unmap calls must not be able to stop it.

## Who the attackers are

| Actor | Can | Today |
|---|---|---|
| A hostile process | Touch its pages from every processor its threads run on, at any moment; hold a translation cached by not entering the kernel; call map, unmap and protect at any rate, in any order, from any of its threads | The main threat. No system call exposes these yet: task S3-5b adds them, and the boot proofs act for the process until then. |
| Two processes that share a frame | One can unmap while the other reads | The same, with the sharing call S3-5b adds. |
| A buggy first-party kernel caller of the framework | Pass a wrong range, or call with a lock held | In scope, as error rather than malice. The API is safe Rust and refuses what it can. |
| A processor that stops answering | Hold up every shootdown that is for it, by a hardware fault or a kernel bug that keeps interrupts off | In scope as a fault. No process can do this: ring 3 cannot mask interrupts. |

**Out of scope:** physical attacks, a kernel already compromised some other way, device DMA (the IOMMU threat model; memory-object frames stay out of IOMMU domains in step 3), and side channels, including speculative use of a stale translation in the window before a shootdown completes (ADR-0004 H5).

## Threats

Status: **Proven**, **Modelled**, **By design** (no proof), **Partly**, **Open**.

### A frame reused too early

| # | Threat | Defence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | A frame is reused while a processor still holds a translation to it, so its next owner's data is read through the old mapping | The page table entry goes first, then a shootdown completes, and only then is the frame released (`Shootdown::retire`). Every access through a cached translation is ordered before the release | **Proven** and **Modelled**: boot proof `unmap_before_reuse` (a frame reused as soon as the unmap returns, holding a secret the old reader never reads); `deferred_free_comes_after_the_last_use_of_a_stale_translation`. The controls that reorder release and shootdown, and that drop the initiator's Acquire on the answers (`initiator_reads_answers_relaxed`, review finding 7), are Loom's: a boot cannot observe either |
| T2 | A frame shared by two spaces is freed while one of them still maps it | Each mapping holds a reference, given up after its own shootdown; the frame goes with the last reference (`RefCount`) | **Proven** and **Modelled**: boot proof `shared_between_spaces`; `deferred_free_of_a_frame_shared_by_two_spaces`, with three controls on the count |
| T3 | A processor that does not answer is left holding a translation, and the frame is reused anyway. Or a change stops half made at the stall, so what its caller records no longer matches the tables (S3-5b's review, finding A) | A frame goes back only after a completed shootdown that covers it. A stall does not stop a change (`Confirmation::change`): every round is still made; the rounds after the stall keep what they take, without a shootdown of their own; and one flush of the whole space at the end gives it all back, or, if that stalls too, leaks it for good and leaves the space unconfirmed. Only then does the caller get `ChangeError::Stalled`, and the change is made | **Modelled**: Loom `a_change_whose_round_stalls_is_still_made_and_frees_only_what_a_flush_covered` and `a_change_whose_first_flush_stalls_is_still_made`, whose initiator gives up after two polls, with five controls (the early return at a stalled round, and at a stalled first flush, as the code was; a stalled round given back at once; what a stalled flush left given back; the stall forgotten). Host tests of more rounds, of refusals, and of a round refused after a stall. No boot proof: no boot can stall a processor on purpose |
| T4 | A frame is unmapped from a space that is being torn down while a processor is still in it | A space's memory is freed only when no processor has it loaded (`tlb::root_loaded`); otherwise it is leaked | **Proven** by the existing proofs of when a space goes |
| T5 | The kernel copies into a page as it is unmapped, and ends up writing into the frame's next owner | The copy runs through this space's own page tables and recovers from a fault at the instruction; the frame is not released until this processor has answered the shootdown | **By design** |
| T28 | A reference count wraps round to zero, so the object or a shared frame is freed under its holders (review housekeeping) | Past `isize::MAX` references `RefCount::retain` reports `TooMany` and `ObjectRef::clone` panics, which halts and never unwinds, as `Arc` aborts | **By design**; a host test drives the count to the limit |

### Stale translations

| # | Threat | Defence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| T6 | A thread keeps using a page after the unmap returned | The shootdown reaches every processor with the space loaded and waits for each | **Proven**: boot proof, on another processor |
| T7 | A processor entering the space as the unmap runs is missed, and caches the old translation | `switch_space` announces the new root, then fences, then loads CR3; `shoot` changes the tables, then fences, then reads who is in the space. One or the other must see the change | **Modelled**: `shootdown_and_a_processor_entering_the_space`, with a control removing the fence on each side |
| T8 | A processor leaving the space is passed over while it still holds translations | A processor counts as holding both roots until its CR3 write is done, and the two are read in the order that cannot miss it | **Modelled**: `shootdown_and_a_processor_leaving_the_space`, with three controls; the third reads the two in the other order (`holds_reads_leaving_first`, review finding 7) |
| T9 | Write or execute access outlives a `protect` | Every change of permissions shoots down, and waits | **Proven**: boot proof `protect_stops_a_writer` |
| T10 | A permission added by `protect` is not yet seen, the access faults, and the fault ends the thread (Intel SDM Vol. 3A §4.10.4.3) | `protect` shoots down for additions too | **Partly**: boot proof passes, but QEMU's TLB cannot show the spurious fault this prevents: **hardware qualification must check it** |
| T11 | A processor is passed over because it was not online yet | The two sides fence, as for T7 | **Modelled**: `shootdown_and_a_processor_coming_online` |
| T24 | The record of a processor's space disagrees with its CR3, so a shootdown passes it over, or a space it runs on is freed: an interrupt between the record and the CR3 write lets the scheduler switch the processor itself (review finding 1) | `switch_space` writes the record and CR3 with interrupts off, and reads the processor and the root it leaves inside | **Modelled**: `a_switch_and_the_scheduler_on_one_processor`, whose interrupt flag is a lock, with the control `a_switch_that_can_be_interrupted` |
| T25 | A space activated outside the scheduler is undone when the scheduler next resumes the thread, so a copy checked against one space runs through another's tables, or a system call returns to ring 3 on them (review finding 2) | `UserSpace::activate` and `vm::activate_kernel_space` take a `trap::Switch`, which only the trap entry makes, for the scheduler's hooks. A proof reads through another space with `UserSpace::probe_read`: a visit with interrupts off, recorded in `visiting` so that a shootdown for that space waits for it, and ended by loading the old CR3 again | **By design** for the token, which the compiler checks; **Modelled** for the visit: `shootdown_and_a_processor_visiting_the_space`, with the control `a_visit_that_is_not_recorded` |
| T26 | A stalled shootdown is forgotten: a retry finds nothing left to change, sends nothing and reports success, while the processor that never answered keeps the old, perhaps writable, translation (review finding 3) | `Confirmation` marks the space. The next `unmap` or `protect` first flushes every processor in it (`Request::NonGlobal`), and only a completed flush clears the mark; the W^X counts of the stalled change stay taken until then. If that first flush stalls too, the change is still made, and the flush at its end decides (T3) | **Modelled** with T3's models: `a_change_whose_first_flush_stalls_is_still_made`, and the control `change_forgets_its_stall`; host tests |

### The kernel half

| # | Threat | Defence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| T12 | A kernel mapping made after a process was created is missing from that process, so the kernel faults while on its tables | Every entry 256–511 of the kernel's PML4 has a table before anything can copy it (ADR-0004 H2); the entries are recorded once and never change | **Proven** under QEMU TCG: boot proof `prove_kernel_half`, with an in-boot control (a space lacking the slot faults) and a control that restores the old behaviour. Review finding 4: on a real processor (CI, AMD EPYC under KVM) the in-boot control did not fault, because the probe page was global and its translation survived the CR3 load. The probe page is now not global, each read drops its translation first, and the proof checks the G bit, which a TCG boot can see (control C24). | T29 | A safe caller builds a space that lacks part of the kernel half, and the kernel runs on it (review housekeeping) | `UserSpace::without_probe_slot` is crate-private, used only inside `KernelProbe::read_without_its_slot`, which visits the space for one read and drops it before it returns | **By design** |
| T13 | The kernel half differs between spaces | Every space copies the recorded 256 entries; the proof compares each space with them, ignoring only the bit the processor sets | **Proven** |
| T14 | A kernel page is unmapped and a stale copy lingers | Unchanged: `Scope::Kernel` reaches every online processor | **Proven** by the TLB proof, unchanged |

### A hostile loop, and contention

| # | Threat | Defence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| T15 | A process maps and unmaps in a loop to interrupt other processors | Only processors with the process's own space loaded are interrupted, so its threads are what it can disturb; the proof counts 0 interrupts on a processor outside the space, and in the model `shootdown_for_a_space_asks_only_the_processors_in_it` interrupting such a processor fails the model at once (control `a_space_request_asks_everyone`, review finding 7). Each call reports its cost (`Change`), and `processors_loaded` prices the next one | **Limited** by S3-5b: each process has a budget of shootdowns and interrupts that its creator sets. An unmap or protect is priced from its pages and `processors_loaded` before it runs and refused with `should_wait` if the budget cannot pay, before any change or turn; its `Change` is charged afterwards (`THREAT_MODEL_SYSCALLS.md` M8, proven by S3-5b's controls 12–14) |
| T16 | Every shootdown in the kernel goes through one turn, so a hostile process's requests delay other processes' and the kernel's | The turn is held only for the round trip, and a waiter serves the request in progress | **Bounded** by S3-5b's budget (M8): a process takes the turn only as often as its creator's rate allows. Per-initiator requests, which would remove the shared turn, are **Open** |
| T17 | A request whose range is huge holds the kernel | A request is walked by what is mapped, not by its size, in chunks of 128 pages with interrupts on between them | **By design** |
| T18 | Mapped-page bookkeeping is slow with interrupts off | A chunk's work is bounded, and the sorted records' largest memmove is bounded by `MAX_TABLES` (about 14,800 pages, under 500 KiB) | **Partly**: S3-5b raised nothing. `MAX_TABLES` stays, each process's mappings are limited by its creator (D4), and the kernel's map, protect and unmap of 16 pages take tens of microseconds under QEMU (S3-5b's figures in `SOURCE_PROVENANCE.md`). Raising the limits still needs measuring first |
| T19 | Two initiators, or a waiter on a space's editor lock, hold up each other's shootdown forever | The space's short lock is never held across a shootdown. Whoever waits for the turn or for the editor lock serves shootdown requests while waiting | **Modelled** for two initiators (`shootdown_two_initiators_serve_each_other`; its control now fails with "a processor the shootdown asked never answered", review finding 7); the editor's waiting loop by design, and its unbounded wait (review finding 6) is unchanged |
| T20 | A kernel caller holds a lock another processor spins on with interrupts off, and shoots down | Documented rule on `unmap` and `protect`. A violation times out as `Stalled`, which leaks; it cannot corrupt memory | **Partly**: the rule cannot be checked by the compiler |

### What a mapping allows

| # | Threat | Defence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| T21 | A page is writable and executable | `Access` has no such variant, and both `map` and `protect` take their bits from one function | **By design**, host tests |
| T27 | A frame shared between mappings is writable through one and executable through another, in one space or two, so it is written as data and run as code (review finding 5) | `Aliases` counts each shared frame's writable and executable mappings in one word. A mapping is counted before its entry is written and given back only after the shootdown that removes the access completes; `map_shared`, `map_shared_run` and `protect` refuse a change that would give the frame both (`ChangeError::WriteAndExecute`), and `protect` and `map_shared_run` check their whole range before they change anything. A direct step from writable to executable is refused too: it goes through read-only | **Proven**: boot proof `shared_frames_keep_w_xor_x`, with the control C25 (a count that refuses nothing fails the boot); host tests, including racing takers |
| T22 | A process maps something of the kernel's | The API maps only frames the kernel gave it, into the user half; `user_range_end` refuses anything else | **By design** |
| T23 | The kernel reads a user frame as a Rust object while ring 3 changes it | Frames shared with ring 3 are reached by volatile copies only (`write_frame`, `read_u64`, `SharedFrame`) | **By design** |
| T39 | A run of pages is mapped in part: a page fails in the middle, and undoing the pages before it needs a shootdown outside the caller's budget, or fails and leaves pages mapped that the caller holds no record of (S3-5b's review, finding B) | `map_shared_run` does all that can fail for the whole run before its first entry is written: it refuses a run over a mapped page, makes the room for its records, makes every page table it needs (`AddressSpace::prepare_user`), and counts every frame's mode. A run refused at the bound gets `ChangeError::Limit`; a want of memory gets `ChangeError::Map(MapError::OutOfMemory)` | **Proven** for the bound: the boot proof fills a space's tables and gets `Limit`; the host test `tablebound` tells the two causes apart. Until the S3-5b session maps `Limit` in `memory_object.rs`, its catch-all arm returns `invalid_args` for the bound |

### The kernel stacks (ADR-0004 Amendment 1, A2)

Every kernel stack the framework makes is in the stack region (`0xFFFF_C000_0000_0000`), in a 128 KiB slot of its own: its pages at the top, the rest never mapped. That includes kernel threads' stacks, each application processor's own stack and its four interrupt stacks, and the TLB proof's pages. The boot processor's interrupt stacks stay in the kernel image and the boot stack stays the loader's (`docs/research/2026-11-10-kernel-stack-region.md` says why). The attackers are the same as above: a buggy first-party caller, and a process whose threads' kernel stacks are made and freed as it runs.

| # | Threat | Defence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| T30 | A kernel stack overflows into another stack, or into memory used for something else | At least 64 KiB below every stack is never mapped, and a frame larger than a page is probed a page at a time (Rust 1.99.1 on `x86_64-unknown-none`, checked), so the first access past the end faults. The #PF cannot be pushed onto the stack that overflowed, so the processor raises #DF, which runs on its own interrupt stack, and the kernel stops with a report | **Proven** for the unmapped space: the boot proof `stacks::prove` reads the page below each stack, the bottom of its slot and the bottom of the next for stacks of 4, 16 and 64 KiB and for each application processor's interrupt stacks (as its TSS names them), and each read faults; controls C26 (every stack's guard mapped, caught by the earlier probes of the application processors' interrupt stacks and of a thread's stack) and C27 (the 64 KiB stacks' guards mapped, caught by this proof). **By design** for the #DF path: a boot cannot overflow a stack and go on |
| T31 | A freed stack's frames or addresses are reused while a processor still holds a translation of them, so a new stack, or another owner's data, is reached through the old one | A freed stack is either kept with its mapping, and then reused at the same addresses over the same frames, so no translation changes; or retired. A retired stack's pages leave the tables, a kernel shootdown of exactly those pages completes, and only then are its frames released and its slot free (`Slots::reclaim`). A stall loses both for good | **Proven** and **Modelled**: the boot proof (another processor's reads of a freed stack fault once it is reclaimed, and its slot is free again, which a slot is only after its frames were released), control C28; Loom `slots_reclaimed_slot_is_taken_only_after_its_shootdown`, with controls `slot_free_before_the_shootdown`, `slot_released_before_the_shootdown` and `slot_taken_while_it_is_reclaimed`. The stall: host tests only |
| T32 | A stack is freed while a processor still runs on it, or while a suspended thread's saved frame is on it | Unchanged: `cpu::stack_in_use` looks at this processor's stack pointer, every processor's TSS RSP0 and every saved frame; a stack in use is leaked (`Slots::leak`), never given back | **By design**, as before; the thread proofs check that the processors hold as many stacks as before the first spawn |
| T33 | Freeing a stack waits for other processors under a lock one of them spins on with interrupts off, so a refused spawn, which drops its stack under the run queue's lock, stops the kernel | Dropping a stack never shoots down and never waits for another processor. Only `stack::reclaim` does, and the scheduler calls it in `reap`, with no lock held | **By design** |
| T34 | A stack's guard is cut out of the direct map, costing a frame and splitting a 2 MiB page for good (what A2 removes) | No guard is in the direct map: the application processors' interrupt stacks and the TLB proof's pages are in the region too, `make_guards` refuses a page below the kernel image, and the boot processor's interrupt stacks' guards are image pages | **Proven**: the boot proof checks that the direct map has every 2 MiB page it was built with; control C29 |
| T35 | A stack page is executable, user-accessible, or mapped outside the region | Every stack page is `kernel_global(ReadWrite)`: writable, never executable, supervisor; `vm::map_stack_pages` and `unmap_stack_pages` refuse a range outside the region | **Proven**: the boot proof checks every page of its stacks' translations |
| T36 | A panic's backtrace faults on the unmapped space above a stack, and the report is lost | For a frame pointer in the region, the backtrace's window ends at the top of the stack's slot | **By design**: a boot does not corrupt a frame chain |
| T37 | Stacks use up the page tables that device registers and firmware tables need | The region's 129 tables are made at boot from a pool sized for them; mapping a stack never takes a table | **By design**; the boot line counts the kernel's tables |
| T38 | Many stacks at once use up memory or the region | 2048 slots; a spawn beyond them is refused, like one into a full run queue; at most 16 freed stacks stay mapped | **By design** |
| T41 | A kernel stack is at the same address on every boot of every machine, so a program that can write kernel memory knows where the stacks are (CLAUDE.md §58, ASLR) | The region's base is drawn when the kernel builds its tables (ADR-0004 Amendment 2, A4, A5): RDSEED, else RDRAND, giving a 2 MiB-aligned base with the whole region in PML4 slots 384–447, about 2^24 placements. A draw never gives the fixed base, which is kept for a boot without entropy, and the boot then says so. The boot names the instruction, never the base | **Proven** in each boot: the stacks proof checks that the base is in the window and was drawn, so is not the fixed base. Controls S1 (a draw that always gives the fixed base) and S2 (a drawn base recorded as undrawn). A host test of 10,004 draws, with controls H1–H3. Across boots: `boot-matrix` reads each boot's base through QEMU's monitor and fails on two alike (A6.4; control S5, a draw that is always the same base) |
| T42 | A boot log or a crash report gives a slide away: the region's base, or an address from which it follows; or the image's or the direct map's after boot (ADR-0004 H3, Amendment 2 A6) | The kernel prints no such base and no address in the region, during boot or after, in any mode: there is no developer mode (A6.1). Crash reports print offsets from the sliding bases (`phys::told`, `layout::tell`): `rip`, `rsp` and the general registers, the panic backtrace and the NMI line (A6.2). The guard probe's boot line names no address (A6.3). The boot report's lines for the image's and the direct map's bases during boot stay, as H3 permits | **Proven**: every boot test scans its whole log and fails on any number in the stack window (A6.3; control S3, the guard's address printed again). The breakpoint's and the NMI's boot lines show image offsets (control S4, `rip` printed as an address). A host test of `tell` |

## What must be closed before real hardware and real programs

1. **T15 and T16**, before S3-5b lets a program call these: a hostile loop must be charged or limited. **Done by S3-5b** (2026-11-10): the budget refuses a change it cannot pay for (M8). Per-initiator requests would remove T16's shared turn; that stays open.
2. **T10**: a spurious fault after `protect` is the one behaviour QEMU cannot show.
3. **T3**: a stalled processor leaks memory silently but for the caller's error; nothing records it (§69).
4. **PCIDs (ADR-0004 H5):** the targeting in `Scope::Space` rests on a CR3 load dropping a space's translations. With PCIDs it does not, and the shootdown must then also reach processors that have left the space, or use a generation per space.
5. **The stack region's slide** (T30, T31, T41). The owner accepted a slide on 2026-11-10 (ADR-0004 Amendment 2, A4–A6; decision sheet item 144).
   - **Built** on `claude/stack-slide`: A4, A5, and A6 as the owner's notes A6.1–A6.4 settle it (items 157–160). That means no developer mode; offsets in crash reports; no region address in the boot log; and distinct placements across boots read through QEMU's monitor (T41, T42).
6. **Device DMA to shared frames:** before a memory-object frame enters an IOMMU domain, it needs pinning and an IOTLB invalidation in the same place as the shootdown (the research ledger's note on `iommu::map`).

## What this does not prove

- Anything on physical hardware (§74, rule 30). Finding 4 was found on a real processor under KVM in CI, and its fix passed there at `81cb61c`. CI's processors are not the declared hardware.
- That an overflow of a running stack ends in the #DF report (T30): a boot cannot overflow a stack and go on. What is proven is that the space below each stack does not translate.
